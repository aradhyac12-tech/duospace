## Construct: partner responsiveness

**Operational definition used by DuoSpace.** Responding to what a partner *expressed* in a way that shows (1) understanding of their stated perspective, (2) recognition of what they said matters, (3) communication of that understanding, (4) a response to the expressed concern or request, and (5) care — while the responder keeps their own perspective, needs and boundaries.

**Evidence.** Perceived partner responsiveness — feeling understood, validated and cared for — is the central mediator in the interpersonal process model of intimacy (S1), and diary studies found it associated with, and partly mediating, the link between disclosure and same-day intimacy (S2: mostly students; S3: 96 married couples, 42 days). S9 reviews work linking high-quality listening to perceived responsiveness; its abstract describes listening and responsiveness as related but distinct, sharing understanding, positive regard and caring, and explicitly discusses implications for constructive disagreement.

**Population / limitations.** S2/S3 are correlational and self-report, mainly US samples; they do not show that prompting someone's reply increases their partner's perceived responsiveness. S9 is a review and theoretical model, not an outcome trial; we read only its abstract. Nothing here concerns AI-generated or app-suggested replies.

## Responsiveness is NOT agreement

Understanding, validation and caring (S1–S3, S9) concern *showing you grasped and care about the partner's perspective*. None of these sources defines responsiveness as agreeing, complying, accepting blame, suppressing one's own needs or avoiding disagreement, and S9 explicitly treats disagreement as compatible with high-quality listening. DuoSpace therefore:
- treats "I understand why this mattered to you, but I experienced it differently" as responsive (tested);
- never forces an apology — an apology appears only when the user asks for one;
- keeps user boundaries, including refusing monitoring requests (location, passwords, phone checks);
- drops user wording that keeps score or guilt-trips, and says why, rather than rewriting the user's meaning.

This distinction is a **design position consistent with** the construct definitions, not an empirical finding DuoSpace has tested.

## Clarification-first and no emotion inference

Rules, not findings: short or ambiguous messages ("Fine.", "Okay.") and anything lacking an explicit request trigger a clarifying question; emotions are never inferred from punctuation, capitals, length, timing, voice or face. There is no validated basis for an app to infer a partner's feelings from such cues, and a wrong inference could escalate conflict.

## DuoSpace may say
"Research on close relationships links feeling understood, validated and cared for with feeling close. This tool helps you reflect back what your partner said, ask when you're unsure, and say your own side clearly."

## DuoSpace must not say
That it improves responsiveness, intimacy, satisfaction or conflict recovery; that it knows what a partner feels or meant; that one person is right; that a reply is "healthy" or a partner "toxic".
